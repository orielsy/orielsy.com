speechBubbles is a single-workstation broadcast prototype built around a simple separation of responsibilities: local inference hears the speech, the client decides what enters the caption queue, the operator controls the graphics, transparent Electron surfaces render them, and OBS composes the final scene.

That separation is the more interesting engineering story than transcription alone. The model runtime does not own presentation, operator controls do not become the on-air surface, and OBS remains responsible for broadcast composition.

## What Worked on One Workstation

The working prototype demonstrated a complete local path from microphone input to on-air graphics:

- microphone-to-caption operation with local Whisper inference
- transcription without a cloud speech service
- operator-controlled captions, lower thirds, and speaker credentials
- transparent graphics composited into an OBS scene

As creator and developer, I designed and implemented the Electron/Vue interface architecture, overlay surfaces, audio capture and transport integration, client transcript policy, local Whisper integration, and OBS workflow.

## Separate Control from Output

The operator-facing application controls what the graphics say: captions, lower thirds, and speaker credentials. Transparent Electron output surfaces carry those graphics while OBS combines them with camera video.

## Move Audio Locally, Keep Acceptance in the Client

The microphone stream is converted to 16 kHz mono PCM and sent over WebSocket to the local FastAPI service. The backend accumulates fixed-duration chunks and runs Whisper inference off the async event loop.

Returned segments and metadata do not move directly into the visible caption queue. The client evaluates them first, keeping transcript acceptance policy close to the UI that consumes it.

That boundary has a trade-off: another client would need compatible acceptance rules or a shared policy layer.

The companion [architecture research](/research/real-time-speech-audio-pipeline-desktop-ui) owns the source-level analysis of chunking, filtering, VAD status, audio-format calculations, and renderer behavior. Those details are implementation evidence, not the center of this case study.

## Design Decisions, Costs Included

Three decisions define most of the prototype's architecture: separating graphics from inference, keeping transcript acceptance in the client, and using Electron for output while OBS owns final composition.

<ProjectBreakout mode="publication">

None of those decisions is free. Separate processes require local setup and coordination. Client-side acceptance keeps presentation policy near the UI but makes policy reuse another architectural concern. Electron and OBS provide a dedicated broadcast surface while tying the prototype to a desktop production workflow.

## Prototype, Evidence, and Open Work

This is a working prototype, not a production distribution. The current source still contains choices I would revisit for sustained use: the legacy `ScriptProcessorNode` capture path, fixed-duration chunking, packaging and setup, automated testing, and the inactive PyAnnote VAD path.

The public repositories were published in August 2026, after the January–June 2024 initial development period shown here. The public history therefore demonstrates the current source, not the original development timeline.

The result is a complete local path from microphone input to on-air graphics with inference, transcript policy, operator control, rendering, and broadcast composition kept as distinct responsibilities.
